The three types of cells that make blood life-sustaining

The Three Blood Cell Types

Blood contains three main types of cells, each produced in the bone marrow and each with a specialized role. Together, they make up about 45% of your total blood volume.

Red Blood Cells

Erythrocytes — the most abundant cells in blood. They carry oxygen from lungs to every cell using hemoglobin, a protein that contains iron. They live about 120 days.

  • Count: ~5 million per microliter
  • Lifespan: ~120 days
  • Function: Oxygen transport

White Blood Cells

Leukocytes — the immune system's defenders. They identify and destroy bacteria, viruses, and other threats. There are five main types: neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, and basophils.

  • Count: 4,000-11,000 per microliter
  • Lifespan: hours to years
  • Function: Immune defense

Platelets

Thrombocytes — tiny cell fragments that clump together to form clots. When you get a cut, platelets rush to the site, stick together, and stop the bleeding.

  • Count: 150,000-450,000 per microliter
  • Lifespan: ~8-10 days
  • Function: Blood clotting

How Blood Cells Are Made

All blood cells originate in the bone marrow — the spongy tissue inside bones — through a process called hematopoiesis. A single type of stem cell, the hematopoietic stem cell, gives rise to all blood cell types. Your body produces about 2-3 million new red blood cells every second to replace old ones.

Every day, your body produces roughly 200 billion red blood cells, 10 billion white blood cells, and 400 billion platelets — all from stem cells in your bone marrow.

When Something Goes Wrong

Blood cell disorders can affect any of the three types:

  • Anemia: Too few red blood cells or low hemoglobin, causing fatigue and weakness
  • Leukemia: Cancer of white blood cells, causing abnormal overproduction
  • Thrombocytopenia: Too few platelets, leading to excessive bleeding
  • Leukocytosis: Too many white blood cells, often indicating infection
